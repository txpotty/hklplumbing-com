    It is a toilet with the seat about 17 to 19 inches off the floor, roughly chair height, compared with 15 inches or so on a standard bowl. Most adults find it easier on the knees. Children and shorter people may find their feet dangle, so for a family bath a standard height with a grab bar can be the better call.

    Watch the base and the tank. Water that reappears on the floor after you wipe it up points to a cracked bowl or a failed seal. We rule out the seal first because it is cheaper, but cracked porcelain cannot be fixed for good, so a crack means a new toilet.

    A one-piece toilet is cast as a single unit, so there is no tank-to-bowl joint to leak. Expect a higher price and more weight. A two-piece bolts the tank to the bowl with a rubber gasket, which makes it lighter to carry up stairs and cheaper to fix, since you can replace just the tank or just the bowl if one cracks. Both flush well.

    Cracks in the tank or bowl are the clearest sign. Hairline cracks can be hard to spot. Watch for a puddle that keeps returning or constant clogs on an early low-flow bowl with a narrow trapway. So is a toilet you have rebuilt twice in three years.

    Adding or moving a toilet almost always needs one. Swapping in the same place usually does not. The permit covers drain sizing and venting, and the inspector wants to see both before the floor or wall is closed, so we schedule the inspection around your remodel timeline.
